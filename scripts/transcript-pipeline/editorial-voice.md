# Henry's episode titles and descriptions

This guide is for **future unpublished episodes**. Existing episode titles and descriptions are references, not rewrite targets. The evidence is the 63 episode frontmatters currently in `src/content/podcast/`; the extracted snapshot is `experiments/transcript-pilot/editorial-examples.json`. These are observations of the supplied archive, not claims about how each item was authored.

## What the archive actually does

**Titles name a particular idea without explaining everything.** Early examples include “Trust”, “Liturgy”, “Community Membership”, and “Holy Inefficiency”. Later examples include “Emotional Programming”, “Artificial Physicality”, “An Ordinary Walk”, and “Internet Checkpoint”. They often carry a productive tension or a concrete image. Guest names or a book appear in parentheses; a subtitle that explains the lesson is usually unnecessary.

**Verbatim is one good source, not a requirement to mislabel every title as a quote.** “Words are Totems” occurs in Xiq's transcript around 25:29 and 25:39. “Holy Inefficiency” is a sermon title Henry brings up around 27:16. “History is Necromancy” condenses David Cayley's discussion of history and necromancy around 17:50; the full title is not an exact phrase in that transcript. “Snow is a Commons” explicitly refers to Illich's essay *Silence is a Commons*. Keep these different kinds of provenance honest.

**Descriptions begin with a real question.** All 63 sampled descriptions include an opening question. Examples: “Can everything that matters be measured?”, “What do we treasure?”, “Why would you choose to leave the public internet on your own terms?”, “Can a conversation have a sense of place?” The question opens an inquiry. It does not promise that the episode solves it.

**Specific details do much of the work.** “An Ordinary Walk” gives Central Park, Memorial Day, sauntercasts, voice notes, and the romance of distance. “Infrastructure as Play” gives Prospect Park, generational mementos, ferries, and a depth jam. “Words are Totems” keeps swarms of AI fairies and the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis. The descriptions do not have to reduce these to an abstract thesis.

**The narrator can be present.** “Laurel and I take a stroll”, “Some themes I liked”, “Just a fun thought”, and “Maybe it's what this podcast is attempting to work towards!” are useful examples. The more recent descriptions often use “I”/“we”, while earlier ones use “joins Henry”. For new copy in Henry's voice, prefer “I”/“we” unless the publication context calls for third person. Do not invent Henry's personal evaluation, a recording location, a mood, or a scene from the audio alone.

**The shape is loose.** Topic lists, parentheses, a specific aside, an unresolved question, and occasional exclamation marks appear. Preserve that openness without manufacturing typos or deliberately imitating every quirk. Some existing descriptions are more abstract; the user's current preference for concrete, spoken language takes precedence over copying all archival habits.

## How to draft future copy

1. Read the corrected conversation first. Note its actual questions, recurring images, and unusually specific phrases. Keep speaker, turn ID, audio time, and surrounding context for each candidate. A memorable line can still be unrepresentative of the episode.
2. Select at most three titles: usually a short verbatim phrase first, then a concise concept or near-verbatim alternative when it is a better fit. Prefer a title that could not describe five unrelated episodes. Do not make provocative isolated remarks sound like the conversation's conclusion.
3. Label provenance internally: **verbatim**, **near-verbatim**, or **concept/reference**. Never use quotation marks or the verbatim label for assembled words that were not said together. Keep attribution when the phrase comes from a book, essay, or someone other than the speakers.
4. Write one plain description: an actual question the conversation raises, who is talking, then a handful of distinctive things discussed. Use exact wording where it fits. A description may be editorial prose; label that internally and cite the supporting passages. A question synthesized from the discussion is not a spoken quotation.
5. Review it beside two or three relevant past examples, not an abstract “sound human” instruction. For a walking episode use *An Ordinary Walk*, *Infrastructure as Play*, and *Snow is a Commons*. For faith/community use *Community Membership*, *Holy Inefficiency*, and *Towards Shalom*.
6. Cut invented conclusions, promotional promises, stock lead-ins, and a tidy lesson the speakers never reached. Do not add “a thought-provoking exploration”, “unlocking”, “a powerful reminder”, or “why it matters” to give the episode significance. If a speaker actually said such words, they remain valid transcript content; this is a rule for generated framing, not censorship of the conversation.

Prefer “We talk about …” or “X and I …” plus the specific things, rather than making every description read like a polished abstract. Do not force a uniform word count across the archive.

## Applying this to the pilots

Episode 1 remains **Faith and Open Source**, with its existing description. The first pilot's alternate title, “You're showing up for a reason”, was a valid phrase but relatively generic and did not foreground the episode's particular connection between faith and open source. Being verifiable was necessary, not sufficient. It is archived as an exercise, not applied metadata.

## Prompt for future episodes

> Use the corrected transcript and these relevant past examples. Find a short phrase or concrete idea that is distinctive to this conversation. Return no more than three title candidates, each with provenance, speaker, turn ID, timestamp, and why it fits the whole talk. Prefer verbatim; label near-verbatim and concept/reference choices honestly. Write one plain description in Henry's first-person editorial voice: a real question, who is talking, and a few specific things they discuss. Preserve uncertainty and digressions. Do not invent a scene, personal opinion, emotional arc, lesson, or resolution. Link each factual sentence to its source passages. Keep the result as a draft; do not alter metadata on an existing episode.
